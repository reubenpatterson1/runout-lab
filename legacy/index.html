Your two leaks · the training plan

You're winning the rack
and losing the close.

You make more balls and clear faster — then the last two balls and the money ball stall out, the opponent climbs back in, and weak safety play lets them off the hook. This lab attacks both with mental reps you do here and drills you take to the table.

Leak 01

The Stall on the Last Two + the 8

Almost always a pattern problem disguised as a pressure problem. Leading early means nothing if you arrive at the end of the rack out of line with no angle on the money ball.

  • Root cause A — problem balls (clusters, pocket-blockers, balls on rails) left for last. Open them early, while you still hold an insurance ball.
  • Root cause B — no key-ball plan. You must pick the last ball before the 8 first, then build the rack backward to it.
  • Root cause C — getting straight on balls, so the cue ball has nowhere to go. You need a working angle on every shot.
  • Root cause D — the mental tighten-up: you start counting the win instead of running your routine.
8
Leak 02

Weak Safeties & Weak Escapes

Defense is just offense that uses your opponent. A real safety forces a foul or a low-percentage shot; a real escape gets you a legal hit and ideally leaves them safe.

  • Setting — the three ingredients of a strong hook: distance + a blocker to hide behind + the cue ball frozen-ish to a rail. Control one ball, not both.
  • Setting — come into the line of the blockers so your speed has margin; don't park the object ball near a pocket or on a rail.
  • Escaping — learn a repeatable kick system (mirror / 2-to-1) so a hook isn't a coin flip — and aim to kick safe, not just to hit.

The closing mindset (read before you play)

Process, not score

Each shot is its own game. When you catch yourself counting the win, that's the signal to slow down and run your routine.

One routine, always

Same look, same warm-up strokes, same final pause — the routine is a stroke anchor that removes variance under heat.

Body calm = cue calm

Slow exhale before the shot, loose grip. Stable physiology keeps the stroke clean when the rack matters.

Zero carryover

Miss happens — say "next play" and move on. The shortest emotional half-life in the room wins close games.

Module 01 · cue-ball control

The reason the last balls linger is almost always cue-ball control. Drag the cue ball and object ball, pick a pocket, and read exactly where the cue ball goes. Master these two rules and you stop arriving straight or out of line.

90° rule (stun)

With a sliding (stun) cue ball, it leaves perpendicular to the object ball's path — straight down the tangent line, every time, regardless of speed. This is your safest, most predictable control line.

30° rule (rolling)

A naturally rolling cue ball deflects only about 30° off its original path for a wide band of cuts (¼ to ¾ ball). Easy to visualize, forgiving on aim — your default for connecting shots.

The fix for stalling

Prefer stop shots and natural rolls to link one shot to the next. Avoid getting straight (no angle = no position). Keep the cue ball on the short side so you always have a route to the next ball.

Module 02 · pattern & sequence

The Closer

Leading early is worthless if you arrive at the money ball out of line. Generate a random layout, pick how many balls you're running, then tap your shot order and grade your plan against a model runout. The tool rewards smooth one-direction flow and a clean key ball — and punishes the zig-zags that strand you on the last two. Hit New random layout for endless reps.

Pick the last ball before the money ball first — that's your key ball — then build the rack backward to it. The money ball is always shot last.

Work backward

Start at the 8 (or 9). Choose the ball that leaves the easiest shot on it — your key ball. Then the ball that leaves the key ball, and so on. You're laying a trail back to your break-down moment.

One side, then the other

Great closers move the cue ball in one direction through a zone, then cross once. Constant up-and-down zig-zagging is where speed errors compound and balls get stranded.

Open problems early

Clusters, pocket-blockers and rail balls are why your last two linger. Break them while you still hold an insurance ball — a makeable ball after the breakout — never as your final act.

Module 03 · defense that wins

Safety Studio

A safety isn't hiding — it's offense that forces a foul or a low-percentage shot. Aim, pick your english, set power, and shoot: the cue ball travels, collides, and rebounds off the rails, then you're scored on where it actually comes to rest — hidden behind a blocker, far from the ball, and tucked to a rail.

Aim with the gold handle on the table, choose your english on the dial (top = follow, bottom = draw, sides = left/right), set power, then Shoot. You're rated on where the cue ball comes to rest.

Ingredient 1 — Hide

Tuck the cue ball behind a blocker so there's no straight line to the object ball. A full hook forces a kick; a foul gives you ball-in-hand — the jackpot.

Ingredient 2 — Distance

Even an open hit is hard from across the table. Most players are weak on long shots, so stretch the cue ball and object ball apart.

Ingredient 3 — Rail

Freeze the cue ball to a rail (or nearly) so they can't use draw/follow to maneuver. Come into the line of the blockers so your speed has margin.

Module 04 · getting out of the hook

Escape Trainer

When they hook you behind a blocker, a kick shouldn't be a coin flip. Each round drops you in a fresh random hook with real blocking balls in the way. Read the mirror line, then aim the cue, choose your english and power, and Shoot — the cue ball is simulated off the rail. You're scored on making a legal hit; a clean two-way that leaves them hooked again earns the most. Ten rounds for a score out of 100.

You're hooked — there's no straight line to the target. Reflect the target across a rail, sight from the cue ball to that mirror image, and aim where the line crosses the rail (angle in = angle out). Then set english/power and Shoot. Tap Show mirror for the line, or Suggest kick if you're stuck.

Mirror / 2-to-1

The workhorse one-rail kick. Aim at the diamond halfway between the cue ball and the target's mirror — angle in equals angle out. Most pros' default escape.

Kick to hit safe

Don't just make contact — try to leave them safe too. A soft kick that returns the cue ball behind a blocker turns defense back on them.

Beyond one rail

For longer escapes, the Plus system (two rails off the short rail) and the famous Corner-5 (three rails off the long rail) extend the same diamond logic.
